abstract: "This work investigates how technical objects and systems support specific spiritual practices, traditions, and forms of social cohesion amidst contemporary ecological and psychic crisis. Drawing on Michel Foucault's late concept of 'political spirituality', Bernard Stiegler's organology and pharmacology of tertiary retention, Gilbert Simondon's theory of transindividuation, and Leonard Harris's insurrectionist ethics, this essay argues that liberatory politics cannot be reduced to material-economic restructuring alone: it requires a pharmacological transformation of attention, temporal orientation, and psychic-collective individuation."

sections:
  - id: "sec-political-spirituality"
    heading: "§1 · Political Spirituality & Practices of Liberation"
    body: "How do technical objects and mnemonic infrastructures shape the horizon of collective liberation? Returning to Michel Foucault's inquiry into 'political spirituality'—the will to discover a different way of governing oneself and others through a transformation of the subject's mode of being [1]—we examine why revolutionary movements falter when they treat technical systems as neutral instruments rather than constitutive milieus of desire and belief."
  - id: "sec-transindividuation"
    heading: "§2 · Organology, Tertiary Retention & Transindividuation"
    body: "Following Gilbert Simondon and Bernard Stiegler, human psychic individuation is inseparable from collective transindividuation, both of which are mediated by technical objects ('tertiary retentions') that externalize memory across generations [2]. When algorithmic and industrial media short-circuit these retentional systems, they induce systemic disorientation and proletarianization of sensibility—a crisis that demands new therapeutic and spiritual practices of care."
  - id: "sec-insurrection"
    heading: "§3 · Pharmacology & Insurrectionist Orientation"
    body: "By bringing Francophone philosophy of technology into direct dialogue with Leonard Harris's 'philosophy born of struggle' and insurrectionist ethics [3], we show that pharmacological critique must be paired with insurgent practices of counter-orientation. At 'the end of the world'—understood as the exhaustion of colonial-industrial modernity's teleology—spiritual traditions and technical experimentation converge as practices of liberation."
